Using GenAI conversational assessment to build student confidence in discussing their science (working title)

Kirsten Knox, Paul Capewell and Martin Rennie

Many students lack confidence in discussing their experiments and thoughts with lecturers. With the advent of GenAI, most institutions are considering introducing oral or viva type examinations to maintain academic robustness, particularly in Honours years. To many students this is a terrifying prospect. To address this issue we developed an assessment for L2 students where a GenAI takes the role of a lecturer for a text-based conversation about their recent experiments in a practical lab. The GenAI is trained to ask questions around specific learning outcomes, so whilst each conversation is unique, similar areas are examined leading to an equitable experience. When students are unclear, or give incorrect answers, the GenAI will encourage and prompt the students to work their way to the correct answer. Following the assessment, the transcripts of the conversations are assessed by staff. Students reported a significant increase in confidence in their ability to discuss their science with an expert and greater understanding of their experimental outcomes. Further, the assessment demonstrated to students a valid way to use GenAI to support their learning and revision.

Virtual Reality peer-to-peer learning: a pilot study investigating peer-to-peer VR supported lessons in Higher Education through a staff-student partnership

Claire Donald, Nicola Veitch (University of Glasgow, SII), Sevinj Rustamova (University of Glasgow, School of Education), Lavinia Hirsu (University of Glasgow, Gabriella Rodolico (University of Glasgow, Imants Latkovskis (University of Glasgow, IT Services), Neil McDonnell (University of Glasgow, School of Humanities)

Virtual reality (VR) is a digital learning tool in Higher Education where users benefit from immersion within interactive environments. Peer-to-peer learning is a well-established pedagogical approach known to improve academic outcomes, social skills, and cost-saving. While both VR and peer learning have demonstrated benefits individually, limited research has examined how these approaches can be meaningfully integrated. This study implemented an evidence-based, peer-to-peer learning strategy within a VR lesson and proposes a supportive framework for its broad implementation within HE teaching. A staff-student partnership was funded by an SEB Education and Research development grant to use a bespoke Disease Diagnostic VR simulation which focuses on quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) to evaluate the research. Student participants were randomly assigned into two groups: those who worked individually through the VR lesson and those who worked in pairs and shared a single VR headset. We investigated the effects each approach had on learning, engagement and student confidence through pre- and post- activity questionnaires. The results of the pilot study will be presented during the session. This pilot study highlights the needs for a structured learning framework to scaffold student learning while participating in VR lessons. These insights inform the development of a pedagogical framework for embedding peer learning in VR lessons which can be evaluated in the future.

3A work in progress

A pilot study exploring the use of self-collected data within a taught workshop to enhance postgraduate student engagement

Sandy Wilson, Michael King, Matthew Hutchinson, and Daniel Kinghorn (all School of Cardiovascular and Metabolic Health)

Traditional pedagogy within sport and exercise science courses often relies on anonymised or hypothetical datasets. However, educational scholarship highlights that active learning, personal relevance, and student agency are central to fostering deeper engagement. Grounded in constructivist pedagogies emphasising learning through doing and reflection, this pilot project investigated whether involving postgraduate students in collecting and analysing their own data enhanced workshop engagement.

Students enrolled in the Technology and Analytics in Sport and Exercise course could choose to wear wrist-worn actigraphy devices to track 24-hour movement behaviours (sleep, physical activity, and circadian measures) and complete related questionnaires at the start of the semester. During a subsequent data literacy workshop, participants who wore the actigraphy devices had the option to use their own self-collected data during multiple tasks, whereas other students used an anonymised alternative. 65% of students (n = 30) chose to collect and use their own data. Of these, seven participants completed an end-of-workshop evaluation on engagement using Likert scale and open-ended questions. Results indicated that using personal data made the session feel more relevant, motivating, and engaging, while making health concepts easier to grasp. Respondents universally favoured this format for future workshops.

Beyond immediate classroom engagement, the project established a wider research pipeline by utilising the shared dataset for multiple summer dissertations, expanding student research capacity and knowledge creation. This student-centred model holds broad relevance for MVLS educators seeking to align practice with the University’s Learning and Teaching Strategy. Moving forward, we aim to iteratively update the workshop and explore scaling this approach across the MSc Sport and Exercise Science and Medicine programme and into other MVLS programmes.

Dyslexia and Coding: Lessons from Experience – what's good for some can be good for all

Jaimie Torrance

Objectives: The teaching of basic programming and data skills is becoming increasingly common not just in traditional computer-science programs, but also in Psychology and numerous other disciplines in higher education as well. There are also increasing numbers of students entering higher education with specific learning difficulties like dyslexia. Yet, despite these growing numbers, and the fact that skills like coding present definitive challenges for learners with dyslexia, there is little empirical research on the topic, or clear guidance for educators. Thus, we set out to investigate what sort of challenges dyslexic learners felt they needed support with and what strategies have helped them in their learning journey (tools, practices, support etc.)

Design: To do this we conducted qualitative interviews with dyslexic learners, to help identify recurring themes in experiences that may help inform pedagogical practice.

Methods: We conducted 21 one-on-one interviews with dyslexic learners, with a variety of coding experience (minimum six months) and a range of educational backgrounds. Transcripts were analysed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis

Results: From this data we present three broad themes relevant to beneficial outcomes; “Community Support”, “Intrinsic Drive” and “Good Practice”

Conclusions: The implications of themes for pedagogical practice will be discussed and related within the context of Universal Design for Learning.

5A work in progress

Psychological Safety Across Veterinary Clinical Rotations: Quantitative and Thematic Analysis of Student Feedback

Luiz Pereira Santos, Samantha Fontaine

Authentic workplace learning is essential in veterinary education, but clinical environments must balance student learning with patient care, service pressure and professional hierarchy. This study evaluated psychological safety across six veterinary clinical learning environments: Small Animal Practice, Anaesthesia, Out-of-Hours Emergency, Diagnostic Imaging, Neurology/Orthopaedics and Internal Medicine.

Data came from 99 student feedback responses using an adapted six-item psychological safety scale, analysed descriptively and inferentially. Internal consistency was strong across rotations (Cronbach’s α ≥ .87). Mean psychological safety scores varied by rotation: Small Animal Practice (4.9), Anaesthesia (4.7), Out-of-Hours Emergency (4.6), Diagnostic Imaging (4.5), Neuro/Ortho (4.2) and Internal Medicine (3.8). Five rotations were in the high psychological safety band, while Internal Medicine was the only moderate-band rotation. Clinical environment explained significant variance in psychological safety, F(5,93)=7.50, p<.001, η²=.29. Post-hoc comparisons showed Internal Medicine scored significantly lower than Anaesthesia, Out-of-Hours Emergency, Diagnostic Imaging and Small Animal Practice; Neuro/Ortho scored significantly lower than Small Animal Practice.

To explain these patterns, 62 free-text comments were inductively thematically analysed using Braun and Clarke’s approach. Two linked themes were identified. First, psychological safety supported learning: students valued environments where uncertainty was normalised, questions were welcomed, staff responded non-judgementally and learners felt included. Where safety was compromised, students described judgement, feeling “in the way” and having to “tread on eggshells”. Second, confidence developed through active participation: students gained confidence when invited into clinical reasoning, supported to take ownership and able to apply theory in practice.

Together, the quantitative and qualitative findings suggest that effective veterinary clinical learning environments combine psychological safety with meaningful participation. The findings support targeted, rotation-specific enhancement of workplace learning culture.

6A work in progress

A Practitioners Reflexive Account of Implementing a Feedback Action Plan in Junior Honours Anatomy.

Caroline Allen, Michelle Welsh

Across higher education, student satisfaction with assessment and feedback is commonly highlighted as a key area for improvement in course evaluations (1,2). Engaging students in feedback improves learning and development of academic skills (3). Strategies to encourage students to act upon feedback has focused on development of feedback literacy, with suggestions including “self-assessment” (4). During academic year 2026-27, we introduced an assessment, feedback and reflection cycle to support students’ engagement with assessment and feedback. This cycle was adapted from the Bristol Assessment Feedback loop shared by Scott Abbot Parterson within the School of Anatomy, University of Bristol.

The feedback action plan (FAP) consisted of a set of questions to promote self-reflection on a skill that was assessed across multiple assessments. After receiving feedback on an assessment, students completed the FAP. Part A was completed upon receiving feedback asking students to identify areas of strength/weakness. Part B was completed before submitting the next linked assessment asking students to reflect on how they used their feedback to complete subsequent work. The FAP was uploaded with their subsequent assessment. This helped close the loop with students enabling staff to comment on whether work has improved or needs continued development.

Students engaged well with the FAP, 86% completing the first FAP, dropping to 57% for the last assessment. The FAP established an open dialogue between staff and students, ultimately creating an iterative cycle of student learning through assessment and feedback. Students were able to identify common feedback themes across assessments which enabled me, as course coordinator, to signpost to available academic support. Further research is required to determine whether students receive any benefit from this practice. This approach, however, showcases an innovative approach to learning through assessment and development of life-long reflective learners.

Developing Psychological Formulation Skills in Medical Students: A Health Psychology Student Selected Component

Dougie Marks

Psychology is central to understanding how patients make sense of illness, engage with care, and navigate behaviour change. However, medical curricula often emphasise diagnostic knowledge and communication skills more explicitly than integrative psychological reasoning. This work-in-progress presentation describes the design and evaluation of a Year 3 MBChB Student Selected Component, Applying Health Psychology to Patient Care, developed to help students apply behavioural science models to real patient narratives.

The SSC is constructively aligned around three core learning activities: preparatory teaching on health psychology, behaviour change and the CBT Five Areas model; supervised group interviews with patient volunteers at the Beatson West of Scotland Cancer Centre; and assessed integration through a written formulation report and reflective video. Students are required to move beyond description of patient experience towards theoretically informed explanation, linking cognition, emotion, behaviour, bodily experience, social context and healthcare interaction.

The scholarship project evaluates how students experience this transition from learning psychological concepts to using them as interpretative tools in clinical education. Data will be generated through student reflective work, evaluation responses and qualitative analysis of how students describe patient experience before and after the SSC. The methodology is designed to explore whether formulation functions as a threshold concept in medical education: a way of reorganising students’ understanding of illness from a primarily biomedical account towards a biopsychosocial and person-centred model.

The project aligns with MVLS priorities around enquiry-led teaching, authentic assessment, patient-centred care, and educational impact on the Learning, Teaching and Scholarship track. It has relevance across Medicine, Dentistry, Nursing and wider healthcare education, where students must learn not only to communicate with patients, but to understand the psychological and behavioural systems shaping health, illness and care.

Immersive simulation for managing farm animal emergencies in veterinary education (working title)

Jayne Orr, Andrew Maxwell and Carolyne Ricardo

Veterinary graduates are expected to manage farm animal emergencies e.g. calvings, in high-pressure and unpredictable environments. There is evidence that simulation improves student competence and confidence for bovine obstetrics. Immersive simulation places participants within a highly realistic, interactive environment, allowing them to experience complexity of real clinical practice while maintaining a safe learning space. When combined with a structured de-brief, it enables learners to reflect on events. We aim to share our experiences of delivering an immersive calving simulation to predominantly final-year veterinary students.

A calving simulator was installed in a farm animal shed, and on-call veterinary vehicle positioned nearby. An actor portrayed the farmer, and one student volunteered to manage a 15-minute calving scenario, with no staff intervention. Cameras and microphones live-streamed the scenario to a separate room, where fellow students and staff observed. Thereafter a structured 30-minute de-brief was facilitated by staff, focusing on clinical reasoning, communication and emotional responses. Student feedback was collected in 2024 and 2025.

Student feedback demonstrated a positive response. Students reported the immersive nature of the scenario enhanced realism and emotional engagement, while the structured de-brief was consistently identified as the most valuable component for learning. Discussion of triumphs and challenges supported reflective practice and helped contextualise clinical decision-making. Challenges included technical issues with camera and audio quality, limited opportunities for all students to act as the primary veterinarian and the need for flexibility in adapting the de-brief.

Both staff and students felt that immersive simulation effectively prepared students for managing calving emergencies. This approach enhanced engagement and depth of discussion beyond traditional simulation formats. Further evaluation is required to optimise technical set-up, determine appropriate group sizes, and explore differences in learning outcomes between active participants and observers. We encourage other subject areas to consider adopting immersive simulation to support authentic, reflective learning.

Collaborative manuscript preparation as an effective approach in ensuring engagement and team working in multi-disciplinary SoMDN Athan Swan working group

Tomoko Iwata, Sue Campbell, Rafael Henry-Venson, Claire Fitton, Caroline M Allen, Konstantinos Gkikas

While importance of organizational response to equality, diversity and inclusion (EDI) is clearly recognised and strategies are put in place (Prince 2023, Toroghi 2024), identifying action plan bottom-up is often challenging, particularly when diverse groups’ interests are involved. Here we report our reflection on the use of collaborative manuscript preparation as an effective way to maintain engagement and to produce reportable output to Athena Swan application and in SoTL.

Based on an outline proposed by the lead member, five members of SoMDN Athena Swan Aspiration and Development working group engaged in regular discussions, with a manuscript’s overall theme as barriers and facilitators towards EDI in Health Professions Education. All members were asked to contribute a chapter, expressing own opinion from mid/early-career LTS, early-career R&T, PGR and LGBTQ+ staff. Benefits of mentoring emerged as a key message encompassing all areas. In parallel, reflective analysis format in JPAAP was agreed as a target, which allowed members to re-evaluate and modify the contents suitable for wider dissemination. The manuscript was also deposited to EdArxiv. The members with more experience in publishing supported those with less experience in responding to reviewers’ comments. The manuscript is currently under review.

In reflection, this approach worked well in encouraging the Athena Swan group members to work collaboratively without losing the momentum, to contribute, and to identify a common theme. Challenges were associated with finding time that all members can meet to discuss, which was overcome by setting realistic timeline and communicating by emails, as an alternative to discussing live. In conclusion, collaborative manuscript preparation towards peer-reviewed journals encourages and supports staff amidst their busy schedules, and can be adopted more generally to SoTL.

Testing the efficacy of virtual reality as a tool for immersive, active learning (working title)

David Simmons, Penelope White, Sarune Savickaite (University of Exeter)

Virtual Reality (VR) technology shows considerable promise as a pedagogical tool but requires further testing to better understand its efficacy in educational settings. Further research is needed to determine whether the promising results obtained in educational contexts are due to the technology itself or a byproduct of its interactive nature. In this study, we investigated whether the use of VR enhanced the teaching of concepts in the psychology of visual perception when compared to two traditional methods of learning with interactive components. To achieve this, sixty participants were split into three groups (lecture learning, VR learning, and video learning) to learn about stereovision. Results showed that significant learning occurred across all three groups, but that one group was not superior to the others in terms of knowledge acquisition. Self-reported engagement level did not differ between groups, and cybersickness was not found to impact participants. These results indicate VR technology to be an appropriate, but not superior, alternative to lecture-style and video-based supplementary activities.

Supporting staff and student wellbeing and mental health in higher education (working title)

Ashley Le Vin, Fiona Dowell, Caroline Smith, Lindsay Stewart

There are increasing numbers of staff/students in higher education reporting mental ill health (Orygen, 2017; Royal College of Psychiatrists, 2011; Slavova & Tarpomanova 2025). The University of Glasgow Learning and Teaching strategy (UofGLTS) 2021-25 and the Universities overall strategy 2025 had core values of promoting and supporting the positive mental health and wellbeing of staff/students, whilst the most current UofGLTS 2026-36 has a focus on student skills and employability. Within this lies resilience, which links with mental wellbeing as the ability to adapt or cope with adversity or stress. Resilience is an increasingly important attribute as almost half of employers flag this as a skill graduates lack (Institute of Student Employers Survey, 2025). Therefore, it is hoped that by improving mental wellbeing, resilience will also improve.

University Wellbeing services offer a range of support including masterclasses and self-help resources. But these often are only available on certain dates, or the sheer number of resources may seem overwhelming or require signing up to external providers, making finding resources difficult.

This study aimed to provide instantly accessible practical wellbeing resources for staff/students within the School of Biodiversity, One Health and Veterinary Medicine. Staff/students were consulted on the types of resources they would like to have access to and their delivery. Nine recorded resources were created, hosted on Moodle and released twice per week. Once released, resources were available to be used as many times as participants wished. Anonymous questionnaires were used to gather data including assessing participant wellbeing, using the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale, before and after using resources. The questionnaires have only recently closed so this is a work in progress, but we hope that from this study, staff/students will show improved mental wellbeing and resilience to deal with the stresses involved in university and daily life.

Postgraduate student perceptions of professional body accreditation and employability (working title)

Carolyn Loveridge, Noreen Siddiqui, Prarthna Clare, Hui Chen, Zannatul Farin

Employability is recognised as a central outcome of higher education, with growing expectations on higher education institutions (HEIs) to produce graduates who are both work-ready and adaptable to a rapidly changing labour market. Professional body accreditation signals alignment between academic programmes and the evolving knowledge and skills required by industry. However, despite membership benefits, there is limited understanding of perceptions at postgraduate taught (PGT) master’s-level concerning how students view accreditation in relation to their employability and if this varies by degree subject. This study aims to address this gap and aligns with (i) the third pillar (Professional and Skills Developments for Students) of the University’s learning and teaching strategy and (ii) other internal learning and teaching development funded projects with a focus on employability e.g. the EEVE (Enhancing Discipline-Specific Employability Skills and Awareness via the Virtual Learning Environment) project.

This presentation outlines findings from qualitative thematic analyses of focus group discussions which were conducted separately with marketing (n = 7) and life sciences (n = 13) students whose master’s programmes are accredited by the Chartered Institute of Marketing (CIM) and the Royal Society for Biology (RSB) respectively. While some different sub-themes were identified between the two cohorts, three common themes were distinguished: motivations, accreditation journey and positioning. The credibility of accredited programmes was noted as a key motivator behind choice of degree programme. Awareness of and engagement with professional bodies were highlighted as key elements in the accreditation journey. Several causes of disengagement were also identified. Participants emphasised the format, frequency and timing of communication regarding membership and hearing success stories of alumni from accredited programmes as being key to disseminating the benefits of accreditation to students. These findings are formulating our ongoing research to develop a framework to enable educators to best position accreditation to benefit student employability.

AI-Ready or AI-Reliant? Exploring Student Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence in Bioscience Education

Leah Martin, Lauren Headland, Rafael Henry-Venson, Louise Miller, Noha Ehssan Mohamed, Libby Paterson, Leighann Sherry, Nicola Veitch

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools are increasingly embedded within higher education, raising important pedagogical questions regarding student learning, assessment, and academic development. This study explores postgraduate student experiences and perceptions of AI within the a range of undergraduate and postgraduate bioscience programmes at the University of Glasgow. The work aligns with the University’s Learning and Teaching Strategy through its focus on digitally informed learning, student partnership, and the development of responsible and critically reflective graduates prepared for evolving professional environments.

The study is informed by concepts of AI literacy, self-regulated learning, and the hidden curriculum within higher education. In particular, it considers how students navigate implicit expectations surrounding AI use in contexts where institutional guidance and assessment practices continue to evolve. The project also draws upon scholarship surrounding assessment authenticity and concerns regarding potential deskilling or overreliance on generative technologies.

A mixed methods scholarship approach was used. An anonymous survey explored patterns of AI use, confidence, perceived benefits, and concerns among MSc students. Follow-up focus groups provided deeper qualitative insight into student attitudes, behaviours, and decision making surrounding AI use in academic work. Quantitative data are being analysed descriptively, while qualitative data are undergoing thematic analysis.

Preliminary findings suggest widespread use of tools including ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and Perplexity. Students reported using AI for topic familiarisation, paraphrasing, structuring assignments, and generating study materials. However, participants also expressed uncertainty regarding acceptable use, inconsistent policy interpretation, and concerns surrounding dependency and reduced critical engagement with learning. Emerging findings suggest the existence of a hidden curriculum in which AI use is perceived as widespread yet insufficiently acknowledged or openly discussed within formal teaching environments.

The study contributes to ongoing discussions surrounding AI literacy, assessment design, and responsible integration of generative AI within bioscience and healthcare education. The findings have broad relevance across MVLS disciplines as educators seek to support students in becoming AI-ready without fostering AI-reliance.

Do Voluntary Formative Quizzes Matter in Large Classes? A Learning Analytics Study of Student Engagement and Summative Assessment Performance

Danila Gurgone

Formative assessments are designed to support learning and enhance performance in aligned summative assessments (Adkins,2018). Despite their recognised benefits, student engagement with formative assessments is often inconsistent (El Yazidi, 2023; Wafubwa, 2020). These challenges are amplified in large-enrolment courses, where formative practices can be difficult to scale and are often underutilised (Hadfield, 2023; Lloyd et al., 2022). Furthermore, evidence on how best to promote meaningful engagement with formative assessment remains limited (Wafubwa, 2020; Kowalski & Kowalski, 2015). This study explores the relationship between student engagement with formative quizzes and performance in aligned summative assessments within an undergraduate biosciences’ module.

This work is approved by the SoTL Ethics Committee (#144). Learning analytics data were extracted from three voluntary formative quizzes aligned to teaching blocks FMB, FS and G2E within the module BIOL2039 (657 students, year 2025-2026). Student engagement metrics were linked to aligned to Class Test 1 (CT1) question performance using pseudonymised student identifiers. Descriptive statistics, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests and Spearman rank correlations were used to explore associations between formative quiz engagement, formative performance and CT1 outcomes. Analyses were conducted in R version 4.6.0.

Formative quiz engagement varied considerably across teaching blocks, with a substantial proportion of students engaging shortly before CT1, indicative of cramming behaviour. Students who used formative quizzes achieved significantly higher CT1 scores than non-users for FS and G2E, while no significant difference was observed for FMB. Among formative quiz users, higher best formative quiz performance was positively associated with CT1 performance for FMB (ρ = 0.183, p < 0.001) and FS (ρ = 0.235, p < 0.001), although these relationships were weak. No significant association was observed for G2E (ρ = 0.040, p = 0.364). These findings suggest that engagement with voluntary formative quizzes may support summative assessment performance, highlighting the potential value of encouraging meaningful and timely student participation.

Using Game-Based Learning to Build a Community of Learners and Enhance Student Engagement in an Online Course

Marika Salonna, Mary McVey

Sustaining student engagement and peer interaction remains a key challenge in higher education, particularly in large first‑year online courses. Game‑based learning (GBL) has been shown to support motivation, collaboration, revision, knowledge retention, and skills development across varied learning environments. This study evaluates the impact of a GBL approach aimed at enhancing engagement with course content and supporting peer interaction in the Level 1 online course Current Directions in Life Sciences (CDLS).

The GBL approach was implemented in response to student feedback highlighting difficulties in building peer connections for group work and engaging with course content, and aligns with the University Learning and Teaching Strategy by promoting active, student‑centred learning and the development of transferable skills. Based on these findings, the activities are being refined and will continue to be used and evaluated within CDLS. The approach will also be extended to in-person first‑year biology courses to evaluate its effectiveness across different teaching contexts, including large cohorts and courses for international students. Future evaluation will broaden the focus to examine how the approach supports student transition and course orientation. While developed within a biology context, the approach is adaptable to other disciplines and student cohorts and is expected to have wider relevance across the University.

Evaluation of Science in Medicine curriculum strand of Year 1 and 2 MBChB

Sarah Meek

This presentation outlines the iterative, evidence-based development of the MBChB Year 1&2 Science in Medicine (SinM) curriculum theme, using student feedback and assessment data to guide improvements. This theme aims to strengthen students’ critical appraisal skills and understanding of medical research methods and statistics, forming a foundation for Evidence-Based Medicine (EBM). These competencies are required by the UK General Medical Council, yet their teaching and assessment vary widely across UK medical schools; the literature shows that limited critical appraisal skills are common in medical students and doctors. Furthermore, broader research and statistical literacy are essential for informed citizenship and recognised as key graduate attributes (UoG Future Skills Framework).

Following its first full implementation, the revised SinM curriculum was evaluated using multiple approaches. A semi-quantitative online survey explored student perceptions of both teaching and their own understanding. Assessment performance on SinM learning outcomes was analysed before and after iterative changes to teaching content and methods. Student engagement with online materials was estimated using Moodle and Echo360 analytics, and its relationship with assessment performance examined. Additional qualitative feedback was collected via Moodle and Evasys.

Initial findings highlight: Timely signposting of online, asynchronous content was important for student engagement. Increased engagement correlated with greater topic emphasis within assessments. Not accessing online SinM materials was associated with failing to sit or failing the end-of-year written examination. Although survey response rates were low, most respondents considered SinM content important and at an appropriately level. However, a substantial minority questioned the relevance of critical appraisal and EBM to their studies and future practice. Despite this, many students reported regularly using primary research articles for Problem-Based Learning, with recommended textbooks used less frequently. Further evaluation is ongoing to inform continued curriculum refinement.

Anxious, excited for the challenge: A mixed methods time-capsule study across a quantitative research methods and statistics course

James Bartlett, Thinh Pham, Ashley Robertson, Wilhelmiina Toivo

Objectives: Psychology students often begin with high anxiety and low confidence about learning quantitative research methods and statistics. Anecdotally, many later report enjoying the subject and performing better than expected. However, previous research has not compared student perspectives at the start and end of a course, and often excludes online learners. This study investigates how confidence, attitudes, and anxiety shift across an introductory research methods course.

Design: We used a mixed methods, time capsule design where students complete quantitative measures and free text prompts at the beginning and end of the course.

Methods: Thirty two face to face and 30 online distance-learning students on an MSc Psychology Conversion degree completed the time one measures. Data collection for time two closes on 3 June 2026. At time one, participants completed quantitative items on concepts including perceived value and confidence, and provided free text responses about anticipated barriers. At time two, they repeat the quantitative measures and we remind them of their earlier free-text responses to reflect on.

Results: Our analysis plan uses ordinal regression to compare quantitative items between time one and time two, to investigate how their perspectives shift over the course. Content analysis of free text responses explores differences in what students emphasise at the start versus the end of the course.

Conclusions: A conversion degree offers a unique opportunity to track perspectives towards quantitative research methods and statistics across two modes of study. The perceived versus actual challenges enable us to alter our course design and share best practice to address those challenges for future students.

Enquiry on the intercultural experiences and challenges of PGT Ethnic Minority International Students surrounding active learning in Higher Education

Prarthna Clare, Noha Ehssan Mohamed

International students constitute a significant and growing proportion of the student population at the University of Glasgow, accounting for 50.9% of enrolments across UG, PGT, and PGR programmes in 2023–2024 (1). Within this context, the University’s Learning and Teaching Strategy (2021–2025) and Graduate Attributes framework emphasise the importance of student-centred active learning in developing independent, confident, and collaborative learners who are effective communicators (2,3).

However, active learning pedagogies may present intercultural and educational challenges for some PGT ethnic minority international students, particularly where prior higher education experiences have been shaped by different teaching and learning norms (4–6). Moreover, existing research frequently homogenises ‘international’ or ‘BAME’ students, overlooking important differences in ethnicity, nationality, and other intersecting factors that shape engagement. Studies are also often limited in scale and institutional scope (7), with findings that may not fully reflect the composition and experiences of diverse PGT cohorts at the University of Glasgow.

This project addresses these gaps by focusing specifically on how ethnic minority international PGT students experience and engage with active learning during their transition into UK higher education. Using a student-centred approach, the study will explore perceptions of challenges related to participation in active learning, including engagement in discussions, collaborative activities, and authentic assessments (e.g. group presentations), as well as associated aspects of academic literacy.

Supporting the inclusion of Chinese international students in UK higher education (working title)

Steph Sayan, Gaby Mahrholz, Wei Ching Chen, Lifan Zhang, William Sjangh Xiang, Ziyi Huang

Chinese students represent the largest international student cohort in UK universities, therefore are a key demographic in the Higher Education landscape. Studies have shown that students from China face unique challenges adapting to UK academic life. These include language barriers, difficulties with social integration, and differing academic expectations. These experiences can lead to negative academic outcomes, including reduced attainment and increased drop-out rates. Gaining a deeper understanding of Chinese students’ experiences is therefore key to developing a more inclusive and supportive learning environment. Despite addressing some of these challenges, existing studies often take a deficit-focused approach, which frame students in terms of what they struggle with, rather than recognizing the diverse strengths, skills, and perspectives they bring to the classroom. Moreover, there is limited research that explores these students’ own views on how best to navigate participation barriers, or that examines how current institutional support may fall short of their needs. Together with four Student Interns who are PGT students from China, two staff researchers are conducting qualitative research exploring barriers to classroom participation, gather students’ perspectives on overcoming them, and identify where additional/more effective support is needed. The overarching aim is to move beyond deficit narratives and develop inclusive, student-informed strategies that recognize and build on the strengths of Chinese students. Interviews are currently being conducted, in Mandarin and English depending on participants preferences, and data will be analysed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis ready to be shared at the conference in August. Presenters will provide delegates with clear, actionable suggestions to make classrooms more inclusive, where all learners can participate.
